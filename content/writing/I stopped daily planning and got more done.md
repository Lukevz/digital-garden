# I stopped daily planning and got more done

[I stopped daily planning and got more done](https://youtu.be/fsKB5qy3yS8)

I’ve pretty much tried every productivity system out there, and after years of experimentation I’ve distilled my workflow down to three main principles. It’s a setup you can use with pretty much any app or tool you’re already using, and it’ll help you stay a lot more focused and prioritized throughout your week.

Most people plan by day and then give up on Wednesday. When you switch to planning your schedule by week, it gives you a lot more breathing room. This system has worked for me throughout the years, whether I was managing design teams or acting as an individual contributor, and it’s worked across multiple tools and job titles. So no matter what you’re using, this is how you can plan your week so you actually get stuff done.

## The three core principles

1. **Keep everything consolidated.** Don’t use multiple apps for the same purpose. It’s a lot easier every day if you have one app for your notes and one app for your tasks.
2. **Make it easy to capture.** Have a dedicated scratch pad for any thoughts, or to jot down notes during a meeting, and use shortcuts and quick entry so capturing a to-do never means navigating anywhere.
3. **Plan weekly, prioritize daily.** These are two separate headspaces. Set up the whole week for success, then each morning pick one of those main things to focus on for the day. You do the prep work up front and then choose from a prepped list, rather than starting from scratch every morning.

## Tasks in Things 3

I use an app called Things 3, and I’ve been using the same task manager for almost three years now. It’s minimalist, gets the job done, and doesn’t add features I don’t need. If you’re mostly handling your own to-dos and not sharing with a team, it works very, very well. I did a whole post on [why I chose it over the other to-do apps](/#writing/the-search-for-the-best-todo-app), but the main gist is that it’s minimal and keeps you focused on the actual work. It’s just a place to keep things organized.

You organize your list with areas and projects, so if you’re familiar with the PARA method this should feel natural. The thing I love most is the Today view. I spend about 95 percent of my time in it. Anything you add for today shows up there, and if you don’t finish a task, it just rolls over to the next day. It doesn’t punish you or yell at you for not getting things done. You can check it off tomorrow.

So the way I’ve adapted it is that I use the Today view as my **week** view. On Monday I look over any reminders and deadlines, then I go through each project and pull backlog tasks into Today for the whole week. If something has a specific due date, I add a deadline so it gets flagged on that day. Things is gracious about the rest. That’s why you have to be intentional with reminders and deadlines if something is tied to a specific date.

I found that starting every day with a blank page and then pulling in to-dos felt like starting my week from scratch every morning. It just wasn’t sustainable, because inevitably I get pulled into a meeting or my energy is lower. I don’t want to have to take in all the data from my calendar and emails and tasks every single day. I can front load so much of it at the beginning of the week and then carry that momentum over each day.

### How I organize it

This has stayed fairly consistent through multiple jobs over the last couple of years. For my 9 to 5, I have one dedicated area, which I now call **Business**. Inside it I have a project for each team I’m on (currently three), plus one for admin and one for side quests. Admin is things like checking email and submitting expenses, the extra maintenance work. Side quests are optional, fun explorations, like helping a coworker with something that isn’t part of my day-to-day.

I don’t add a project for each actual project at work, because then you’re tying the completion of that project to your success at work, which isn’t controlled by you. Rather than managing projects in here, they’re almost like sub areas. Recently, because my new job has a lot of smaller projects that I like to bundle together, I’ve started using tags inside those spaces. In the Today view, I can hit a project’s tag at the top and filter down for a bit, which is a nice way to switch between a bird’s eye view and, say, being in a meeting about that project. I only do this a couple of times a week, and it saves me from rewriting what the project is in every task name. I also have a tag for **waiting**, for when I’m waiting on a customer or teammate, so I can use it for follow-ups. I’ve tried tags for priority in the past, but it honestly got to be too much. When I’m adding tasks with quick entry it’s really quick, and I don’t have time to fill all that out.

Outside of work I have a few areas:

- **Life admin** is a catch-all for paying bills, running errands, and anything that’s more of a chore around the house, so it doesn’t feel like it’s personal or work. If something bigger comes up I’ll create a project. Tax season is a recurring project that pops up every January, so I always have that checklist organized.
- **Personal** is the same idea, a nice catch-all for things that aren’t time-sensitive. When something big comes up, like planning Valentine’s Day, I can put a bunch of tasks under it and close it out as a project. I try to avoid creating projects too easily. I usually start with a task and add subtasks, and only when it gets too disorganized do I convert it to a project. Things makes that easy: you can convert pretty much anything into a project by right-clicking, so you don’t have to start over.
- **Labs** is a catch-all for any experimentation I’m doing, especially in the age of vibe coding. I feel like I come up with an idea every single weekend.
- **Vlog** is where all of my video ideas live, listed as tasks. Once I’ve committed to a video for that week I convert it to a project and break down the stages: B-roll, editing, publish and schedule.

The key with all of the non-work areas is that I like to push them to the bottom of Today. I could drag them there manually, but I built an Apple Shortcut that looks for any task in those areas and marks it for **This Evening**. Things automatically has a section at the bottom for that. It’s less that I do them at night, and more that they’re grouped together as personal, even though they’re spread across different areas and projects. I really wish I could schedule this automatically in Things, but it’s not a feature, so it’s a little tedious that I had to build it. Once it’s set up, you never have to come back: every morning the work stuff is on top and the personal stuff is at the bottom.

### Keeping completed tasks around

Recently I’ve been keeping completed tasks in the same view, and I really like it because you feel momentum as you check things off throughout the week. In Things there’s a setting under General for when completed items move to the Logbook. You can have it happen immediately, at the end of the day, or manually, and I’ve set it to manual. On Monday I get these three things done, Tuesday I get this stuff, and it’s a little dopamine hit to look at the list and see that I’ve checked off a lot, rather than just seeing more white space. It feels a little bit more celebratory.

Then on Friday at the end of the workday, I hit Log Now to clear out my work tasks and only go into the weekend with my personal stuff. If I have outstanding work tasks, I set them due the following Monday, so I don’t see them on a Saturday or Sunday.

## Notes in Bear

My notes app has changed quite a bit through the years. I’ve bounced between Notion, Apple Notes, Craft and Obsidian, in this never-ending quest to build a second brain. Part of the change was switching jobs and having quite a few things in my personal life change: living in a house where we had a lot of documentation that needed to live somewhere, working with my partner on shared notes in Apple Notes or Google Docs, and having the apps I use at work change with company policies.

One of the reasons I struggled is that I was trying to set up something that would work for my job, for my personal life, and for basically all the note-taking in my life. What I realized is that it’s best to use whatever your company uses for work, and then figure out what works for your personal notes. I just can’t use Notion for all of my personal stuff. I find it way too complicated, and for a lot of what I’m doing with AI these days I really want markdown-formatted notes. That’s where Bear works well. My most recent setup, which I’ve been running for the last six-plus months, uses Bear for about 90 percent of my notes. I love how minimalist it is, and it plays nicely with Things, since I can copy links between projects and notes.

For weekly planning it’s super simple. I have one note for the week, titled “Week of” and the date of that Monday, tagged to my business tag, since this is almost always a dedicated note for work. It has three sections: a **scratch pad**, a **log**, and **goals**.

### Goals

This is how the week starts. I list the key things I’d love to have done by Friday. I’ll look over my meetings for the week, any conversations I’ve had, and notes I have in Notion from colleagues, and over the next 10 to 20 minutes distill it into key focus areas. This is different from my tasks in Things. These are the main things and impact I want to have over the week, so I don’t have to keep this in sync with my task list. It’s setting my intentions, and I mostly look at it at the end when I’m doing my reflection.

### Log

This is where I do a brain dump at the end of each workday, which I talked about in my [9-5 reset guide](/#writing/how-to-separate-work-from-home-the-9-5-reset-guide). I literally turn on Wispr Flow and talk through everything I got done. I have a Monday to Friday heading for each day. At the end of the week I highlight all of it, copy it into something like Claude, and ask for a really concise summary. I’ll take a bulleted list of the actual action items and paste it into our shared team channel, where we all post what we got done for the week, and I save the fuller reflection into my business journal.

### Scratch pad

This goes back to the second principle, making brain dumping easy. In Things I can hit control-space no matter where I am on my computer and it pulls up quick entry. I rarely take notes in my to-do app in meetings. I enter tasks that way and they’re immediately set to today, so they get added to my week automatically.

For Bear, I always have this week note pulled up on the left third of my ultrawide monitor, with the scratch pad at the top. Whether I’m in a meeting and need to jot something down, or I’m working through something before I jump into Claude or ChatGPT, this is always where I start. If I’m brainstorming, I’ll quickly write the pain point and some ideas, and then that becomes an outline I can put into Claude. That makes it much, much less stressful than navigating to different apps and pulling up the right tab.

I cycle through the scratch pad three or four times a day and clear it out at the end of the day. If I got in the middle of something, that’s fine, I’ll leave it. But it’s rarely something I come back to reference. I’ll jot down my notes, refine them, and move them into a more structured place. If it’s worth keeping, I go through Claude and put it in my company’s workspace, which is in Notion. Most of the time, if it’s not worth keeping, it doesn’t need to live anywhere. So many of those are what I’d call fleeting notes, which just don’t need to be referenced ever again.

### Today’s focus

A newer thing I’ve started, since I use the Today view in Things for the whole week, is a place to dump my intention and priority for that specific day. On Monday I’ll write down maybe weekly planning, if I don’t have anything big, but generally by the end of Monday I already have Tuesday’s goal written down, and so on. If I can’t get anything else done, that’s what I need to accomplish.

### The Friday reflection

At the end of the week I go through a quick reflection, building on the last five days of activity log:

- What went well?
- What didn’t go well?
- What am I proud of?
- What ideas do I have?
- What am I dreading for next week?
- What am I looking forward to for next week?

## No system is perfect

Everything here is pretty work-focused, and the main reason is that I don’t take a lot of notes in my personal life. Outside of work, note-taking looks like writing video scripts or the occasional blog article, and those live in their own notes. If something comes up that’s random, like a shopping list or a note I share with my wife, we typically use Apple Notes, so it doesn’t need to be in here anyway. On the rare occasion I want to meet with a colleague or watch a video that isn’t work-related, I’ll create a linked note with a double bracket, like “coffee with so-and-so.” But I very rarely put any important notes in the main week note, because it’s really just meant to be a quick capture that links out to the right place.

That’s generally been my policy, refined over the last couple of years: no system is perfect, no app is perfect. Bear solves 90 percent of my problems, and it’s really joyful to write and work in, so that’s what I’ve chosen. I can use whatever I have to for work, and Apple Notes fills the gap for other personal life stuff.

I hope you realize that you don’t need a fancy, automated or complicated system. Definitely use technology where it helps, but there’s something to be said for taking 20 or 30 minutes at the start of your week just for yourself to think through what you want. All of this could be done on pen and paper. You could have a notebook open with your tasks on the left and your notes on the right.
