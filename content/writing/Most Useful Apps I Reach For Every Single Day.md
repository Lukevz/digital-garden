# Most Useful Apps I Reach For Every Single Day

[Most Useful Apps I Reach For Every Single Day](https://youtu.be/yBK83Bvr-kM)

Here are 10 apps that I use every single day, for work and in my personal life. Every one of these is solving a problem for me in some way, whether it’s making my life easier or helping me stay focused. That includes one app I only discovered about a month ago that’s probably going to go down as my app of the year.

Quick disclaimer: none of these are sponsored, and they’re not in any particular order.

## 1. HazeOver

What this app does is dim all of the windows and apps around whatever you’re focused on on your Mac. You hit a simple keyboard shortcut (mine is ctrl-alt-cmd-H), and whatever app you’re in becomes the center of attention, with a subtle overlay around it. I like it on a dual monitor setup, because I often have a ton of apps open that I don’t necessarily want to close when I’m going into a more focused mode. I can quickly toggle in and out, and it dims those screens when I don’t need them.

There’s a good amount of customization. You can set the intensity of the overlay anywhere from a faint haze to full black, and how quickly it applies, either instantly or fading in. It works really well with two monitors, whether you want a unique window per monitor or just one app for the whole setup. I like the shortcut workflow because I don’t have to click around in the menu bar or open the app. Whenever I’m ready to switch into deep work mode, I hit the shortcut and I’m good to go.

## 2. CleanShot X

I’ve been using this screenshot tool for almost five years now, and it’s the best screen recording and screenshot app around. I have it through my Setapp subscription, and again, not a sponsor, but there are tons of great tools in there, so I’d recommend going that route rather than paying for CleanShot directly.

There are a couple of main reasons I love it. When you take a screenshot, it doesn’t pop up and then disappear like the native screenshots on Mac. It stacks up as many as you take, pinned in the corner, and you can click in and out of any of them to apply different edits. I use this a ton at work to make annotations, or just to make pretty mockups of our UI screens. If I’m throwing one into a presentation, I can add a nice bit of padding and round the corners, and I always get compliments about why the screens look so good, or how I’m able to do it so quickly. It’s because of CleanShot.

I use it for screen recordings too, like a quick demo for coworkers. You can trim the start and end, and since the retina quality on a professional Mac setup is ridiculously high, you can compress a recording down to something more reasonable like 1280 by 720. You don’t need to be sharing videos that are 4,000 or 5,000 pixels wide, and it uploads faster and loads better, with no processing time. If you connect it to their cloud service, which is included in your subscription, you can upload immediately so nothing is saved to the desktop. I have it set up with a custom domain, so every link looks a little more professional and branded.

## 3. Granola

This is my go-to meeting transcription app. I’ve tried a ton of them: Notion AI, Google Meet, Gemini. For some reason Granola has stuck with me, I think because everything comes into one place. It’s easy to see all of your transcripts together, rather than going through a database or your Google calendar, and it triggers on anything, whether that’s Slack, Zoom or Google Meet. You can also chat and ask questions across any of your meetings. It’s good at capturing multiple speakers, and you can connect it to your calendar, so any time a meeting pops up you can join and begin transcribing immediately.

I used the free plan for about nine months without any issues. The only limitation is that your notes don’t save past 30 days, so if you’re good about offloading notes every couple of weeks, you shouldn’t run into any problems. For anything deeper I’ll paste transcripts into Claude. I recently upgraded because I wanted as much history as possible, in case I want to go back and pull out a specific quote from a meeting a few months ago, and I found I needed some of the more advanced models to ask specific questions. Other than that, you can pretty much get away with the free version.

I’ll typically use the chat to ask very specific questions about a meeting if I can’t remember what was said, or I’m trying to get the specific wording someone used. At the end of the week I’ll ask it to look over all my meetings and list every single action item, whether it’s mine or someone else’s on the team. It’s really nice to see everything we talked about in the week, and I do a final check before I wrap up.

## 4. Meeter

This is a simple way to track the meetings and calendar appointments you have for the day. It lives in your menu bar, and when you pull it down it lists whatever’s on your calendar, and you can join a meeting immediately if there’s a link attached. It plays nicely with all the calendars and all the meeting tools. I’ve been using it for about three or four years and I’ve almost never had issues. It does one thing and does it well.

Because I’m using Granola to join and start transcribing now, I pretty much just use Meeter as a countdown to the next meeting, but it’s still really nice to have it up in the menu bar and not have to pull up my calendar.

## 5. Text Sniper

This is a simple OCR tool that lets you take a screenshot of any part of the screen and captures whatever text is in it straight to your clipboard. I do think the native screenshots on Mac and iPhone let you select text, but you still have to take the screenshot, go to it, and highlight it. If you already know specifically what you want to capture, you can skip that step entirely. You don’t have to save a file, and you get selectable text immediately.

This is great when someone has sent a screenshot, or they’re sharing their screen, and I’m trying to get that text into Claude, or paste it into a text layer in Figma without bringing in all the formatting or retyping it manually. It’s become a natural part of my workflow. I have it mapped to cmd-shift-2, right next to my regular screenshot tools, and I use it every single day.

## 6. Wispr Flow

This is my dictation tool, and I think it’s really, really worth the investment, especially if you use a ton of AI tools. Even if it’s just Claude, but especially if you’re talking to Cursor or you write a lot of emails, I’m using my keyboard a lot less. When you’re working from home at your desk eight-plus hours a day, it’s really good to give your muscles a break and use your voice sometimes, rather than always being in the same position with your mouse and keyboard. It’s saved my wrists and hands across all of the AI tools I use for work and personal.

It does a good job fixing mistakes live, so you don’t have to do it in bursts. You can turn it on for 5, 10, 15 minutes and keep talking, and even if you mess up a ton or have a bunch of filler words, it takes all of that out. If there are specific words it doesn’t understand, like your internal company products or people’s names, when you fix one it pops up and asks if you want to save it to your custom dictionary, so it doesn’t keep making that mistake. I rarely open the app itself. I map it to a key to talk, and another to lock it on, and it just works in the background in Slack, email and my AI tools.

## 7. Bear

This is my note-taking app of choice. I technically use other note-taking apps alongside it. I’ll use Apple Notes for quick capture, if I need to write down someone’s phone number or random thoughts throughout the week, and for notes I share with my wife. But Bear is where I like to go to write and focus. If I’m prepping an outline for a video, or brainstorming thoughts for a bigger project, I like that to be in a very focused and simple space, and Bear does that really well. You can go into a focus mode that pulls up just the note you’re working on. Everything is powered by markdown, which makes it really easy for AI to work with.

They also just released an MCP, which means Claude can now look through all my notes and have a lot more context when we’re talking. If I’m chatting with Claude and want to save something, I can push it to Bear without copying and pasting manually.

The main thing, after a year and a half of usage, is that Bear is not something you can customize much beyond the theme and font size. There are no plugins, and there’s not a lot of organization, since everything is tag-based and there are no folders. That’s what keeps me focused on actually writing and getting things done. Something like Obsidian sent me down a rabbit hole of always customizing, always finding new themes and plugins.

## 8. Mist

This is definitely my app of the year so far. It’s a minimal nutrition tracker powered by AI. I used to use MyFitnessPal (at one point I had a 1,500 day streak), and I tried some of the competitors, but a lot of them were bloated with other features, and if you didn’t pay for them they often had ads. What I like about Mist is that it’s very simple to look at. There are no ads and not a ton of UI. It really looks like a simple log of your day.

There are a few ways to capture. You can start typing or dictating immediately, and you don’t have to worry about separating things out. It’s usually pretty good at identifying the individual parts of your meal, splitting out the calories and macros for each item and totaling them. It does a web search and looks things up based on whatever you type, so you don’t have to worry too much about getting everything exactly right. You can also scan barcodes or take photos of a nutrition label. We often use a meal delivery service, and I’ll take a picture of the label on the box, and it pretty much gets it right 99 percent of the time. Accuracy matched MyFitnessPal in my tests.

There’s a chat feature too, which is useful for looking at specific patterns and getting recommendations. It’s all powered by AI, so take it with a grain of salt. It’s meant to be another data point and shouldn’t be used as medical advice by any means. But I’m getting way more value out of this than out of MyFitnessPal, and if you’re serious about tracking your nutrition, I’d really recommend trying it, because it’s made the habit of capturing what I eat so easy.

## 9. MyMind

This is my bookmarking, second-brain app, if you will, and one of the most beautiful apps I’ve used. You can save anything from the web. Use the Chrome extension, hit the little plus, and it saves the whole article or whatever you’re on. You can also use the share sheet on iPhone, so if you see an Instagram video or a tweet you want to save, send it to MyMind and it’s laid out in an easy-to-follow visual layout. Think of it as the Pinterest of bookmarking apps, but with no ads and a lot smarter.

One of the main selling points is that you never have to add tags or organize things manually. They automatically categorize and tag for you so you can search later, so it’s remember everything, organize nothing. They’re always rolling out new features, like note-taking, an MCP, and better AI search. After a couple of years of usage, I find it works really well for capturing anything visual. If you’re an interior designer, or you love fashion, or you’re putting together a mood board, it works very well. That’s not to say you can’t save recipes or text-based stuff. I just think that when you’re scrolling through something, getting a bunch of inspiration at once works really well for my brain. As a designer trying to capture patterns and trends, being able to quickly save something and not dig through folders or tabs to find it later makes it much more enjoyable to use.

I also love the Serendipity feature, which lets you look over 25 random cards you’ve saved and decide whether to forget one or keep it. This is the feature that kept bringing me back to MyMind. I tried other bookmarking apps like Raindrop, and there was never a good way to cycle through your vault, so it was easy to keep throwing things in and never go back to check if you actually needed them. The couple-second delay in the share sheet is friction I actually like, too. It’s a quick test of whether something is really worth saving.

## 10. Swish

Finally, a window management tool for trackpad users. It’s a simple window manager that lets you use gestures on your trackpad to move windows around and lock them into a grid. I’ve used it for almost 10 years. You can customize the grid to quarters or thirds, I think even sixths or eighths, but on a dual monitor setup I only use halves or quarters. If you want two windows side by side, you go up to the menu bar and swipe up into the left, and it pins the window to the left half. If you want one in the top right, you start to swipe and a little grid pops up, and you move around until you get to the quadrant you want. You can swipe up to maximize, or double tap to center a window perfectly on your monitor. It’s great for rearranging apps fast across my two 27-inch monitors.

On a MacBook you have a built-in trackpad, so it works really well there too. Even on a smaller display, it still comes in handy for quickly maximizing screens or doing a one-third, two-thirds split.

So those are 10 apps I use in my workflow every single day. I hope there was at least one in here you hadn’t heard of and want to try out.
