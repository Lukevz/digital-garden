# I Spent Weeks Testing These 5K & 6K Monitors - Here’s the Winner

[I Spent Weeks Testing These 5K & 6K Monitors - Here’s the Winner](https://youtu.be/MAvzIbSeqAA)

This is my experience with four different 5K and 6K monitors. I recently went through a ton of research and testing to figure out what would be a better upgrade for me coming from an ultrawide display. For context, I’m a product designer, so I do a lot of creative work and a lot of work with color. I really need good accuracy and good clarity. There are a ton of monitor reviews out there, but I didn’t feel like many of them talk about real-world use cases and what it’s actually like to live with these displays. Some of these, like the BenQ, are only three or four weeks old, so I did have to take a risk and just try it. This is definitely a different post from what I normally make, but since I was doing this anyway I figured I might as well write it up.

I’ll tell you right off the bat that the one I’m keeping is the 27-inch 5K BenQ, and I’m going to order a second one for a dual setup. That’s what works for me, and it’s not really what this is about. I figured I could help someone else make a purchase if you’re looking at one of these specifically.

## What I was using, and what I do

I originally had a 34-inch flat ultrawide from Gigabyte. It was basically a 2K, 1440 display. From there I invested in my first big monitor, the Apple Studio Display, when it came out. I had it for about three years, and it is still, to this day, the best monitor I ever had. I sold it a little less than a year ago because I felt like I didn’t have enough space. At the time there weren’t a ton of 5K and 6K monitors on the market like there are today (it’s a much better time to be buying one of these), and my options were to sell the Studio Display and get another monitor, or buy a second Studio Display, which I didn’t have the budget for. So I traded it in for the LG ultrawide, and used that for about nine or ten months.

Now I’m having the opposite problem. I had the real estate, but I’m doing even more color work and staring at my monitor for long periods of time, and I really wanted that high-definition retina look while I worked.

## The four monitors

- **LG 40-inch 5K2K ultrawide.** What I’ve been using for about nine or ten months. It’s not a true 5K. It’s 5K horizontal but 2K vertical, so you’re not getting the same pixels per inch as the others.
- **ASUS ProArt 32-inch 6K.**
- **ASUS ProArt 27-inch 5K.**
- **BenQ 27-inch 5K.** The newer 5K from BenQ, the PD version (MA270S), which came out at the beginning of March.

I also compared them to the Apple Studio Display as a reference. The sweet spot for macOS software scaling is about 220 PPI. That’s the goal number, and it doesn’t matter if you don’t care, but a 27-inch 5K or a 32-inch 6K hits it. They’re all 60 Hz except the LG at 72, and I don’t really notice a difference. I think it’s kind of gimmicky. They all have power delivery included. You can only daisy chain with the 32-inch ProArt or the 27-inch BenQ, which means you plug your MacBook into the monitor, plug a second monitor into that one, and only have one cable coming into your computer. That makes a huge difference, especially with power delivery and accessories. The ports are great on all of them, ironically except for the most expensive, which are the Apple monitors. You have to factor in getting a really good dock or dongle, since you can only really use one or two of the Thunderbolt ports. On these, you can use USB-A, USB-C, and an audio jack, so it becomes the dongle that’s built in.

I didn’t try the Koorui 27 or 32-inch 5K and 6K monitors. Koorui is a Chinese manufacturer that took the LG UltraFine displays and started making glossy versions, like you’d see on the 5K iMac back in 2017, repackaged in a very Apple-esque aesthetic. There are a lot of mixed reviews on the quality, some production issues, and you’re paying tariffs since it’s usually coming from another country. I just didn’t want to take that risk, so I stuck with trusted brands: ASUS, BenQ and LG.

## LG ultrawide

The good is that there’s a ton of horizontal room, and it’s great for multitasking. A one-third, two-thirds split, with a secondary notes app on the left and my primary canvas (Figma, Cursor or my browser) on the right, was a really good workflow for me. The colors are pretty good, but it’s a pretty dim display, something like 350 or 400 nits, and coming from a MacBook, or especially from the Studio Display, it’s a noticeable step down. I cranked the brightness to around 80, the most I was willing to go without sacrificing some of the quality and range of the colors, and you have to run the MacBook, which has an incredible display, at 50 or 60 percent brightness to make them look the same side by side. Over time I realized I can’t accept less than retina. I gave it my best shot.

The second gripe is screen sharing. Being able to share my entire screen is a big perk for me, but most of my coworkers don’t have ultrawide monitors, so I’ve ended up sharing one window at a time, and switching back and forth got tedious.

If you care about productivity and maybe a little light gaming, you don’t need a high refresh rate, and you don’t obsess over retina sharpness, the LG is probably your best bet. It’s a middle-of-the-road budget option that does a little bit of everything. But be warned: if you share your screen any amount of the time, you’ll probably want a secondary display or have to settle for sharing one app at a time.

## ASUS ProArt 27-inch

It’s just not that good. On paper it’s a sharp 5K panel with all the specs you might see on the Studio Display. But the thing I’ll tell you up front about both ProArts is that the matte finish and the coating are so bad they take away so much of the clarity and sharpness. They call the technology Lux Pixel, and the marketing says it replicates the look and finish of a piece of paper. Coming from an Apple display, where I want everything to feel smooth, like liquid glass, it felt like someone had taken their fingers and smeared them all over the monitor. You get an almost oily, fingerprint-type look. At some points it’s not noticeable at all, but when you’re looking at solid whites or any solid surface, it has a rainbow, sparkly look. This is a common thing. It’s not just a bad unit. I looked around a lot on Reddit and other reviews, and a lot of other people said the same thing. I think that’s the trade-off for how much you’re saving, and this is a $700 monitor.

I also had trouble with the audio jack: I was running a set of desktop speakers through it and got a really weird distorting effect, and I tried everything I could think of. The speakers on the ProArt are really horrible, too. ASUS has a display widget you can download to control brightness and volume, and it works okay, but it was a little buggy. When I unplugged, which I’m often doing with my laptop, it wouldn’t load, so I’d have to restart the app and wait for it to sync, and the settings would jump ahead from a third to two-thirds to full, so I wouldn’t rely on it.

I really wouldn’t recommend the 27-inch ProArt to most people. There are better 5K options out there, and even settling for a glossy 4K, or one with a better matte finish, would be my recommendation. They’re on sale, so you could almost get two for the price of the 32-inch. But the matte is not usable.

## ASUS ProArt 32-inch

Things are a little different from my experience with the 27. A bigger monitor at 6K just feels better. It’s brighter and a lot more immersive, almost to the point where the matte finish doesn’t matter. I used it solidly for four or five days straight, which was my litmus test to see if I still noticed the finish after a couple of days. On day four or five I still found myself almost getting a headache trying to look at specific text or areas of the screen, because it looked blurry or fuzzy. As a designer who needs to look at solid surfaces and get really accurate colors, it messed with me too much. There’s a pink type of haze, depending on the color behind it, and that rainbow sparkle. Apparently it has a lot to do with the coating and how light is refracted so you don’t get glare, but it takes away from the sharpness and color quality.

The good is that 32-inch 6K feels amazing. I wish, wish, wish I could have kept this monitor. I even hooked it up again before writing this and played around with it for another hour, and I couldn’t look away from the grain. I just can’t move past it. You also get so much more height than I’ve ever had. I’ve only had the 27-inch or an ultrawide, and having that vertical height, and the lack of glare, was really nice. I have a huge window in my office, and being able to look at it from any angle and have it be consistent was a huge plus.

I’d recommend it if you value size and resolution above all else, if you don’t care too much about matte versus glossy, or you even prefer matte, or if you have harsh studio lights and can look past the grain. It’s around $1,000 to $1,100, depending on where you look, which is a huge cost savings from a Studio Display, and especially from a Pro Display XDR.

## BenQ 27-inch 5K

This one has a coating finish BenQ calls Nano Gloss, which is supposed to be pretty similar to Apple’s Nano Texture, but it feels incredibly glossy. There’s some glare, but honestly it’s minor. I think I only noticed it because I had all of these matte monitors standing right next to it. After about three days and probably 30 hours logged, I really didn’t notice the glare. What I really didn’t notice, in a good way, was the finish. I used the ProArt for about five days and was still complaining about it. After one or two hours on the BenQ, I completely forgot what was happening with the monitor. I was very immersed in Figma, just working with colors. I had my 14-inch M3 Pro MacBook Pro open next to it, which is glossy, and they look so similar side by side that it was incredibly easy to forget about it and do the work.

I love all the ports, and you can daisy chain. I didn’t have any issues with the audio jack. It’s much easier to justify than an Apple display. It came in at just under $1,100, which is crazy, because that’s basically the same price as the 32-inch ProArt. It really comes down to trade-offs.

The color looks absolutely incredible. It’s marketed as being incredibly similar to the MacBook displays, and in their settings panel there are a ton of profiles, one of them an “M book” color setting meant to sync automatically to whatever MacBook you plug in. I plugged it in, it said it was in sync, and pulling them up side by side I could hardly tell the difference. When I first plugged it in, I thought I noticed a subtle vignetting, maybe 5 to 7 percent darker in the corners. It could have been the profile I was on before I switched, but they also have a uniformity toggle that gives up a little color accuracy for an even brightness across the monitor. I felt like it made a difference, but once I got into it I didn’t really notice the vignetting either way.

The downside is obviously that it’s 27 inches. I thought I could live with just one coming from the ultrawide, and it’s quite an adjustment, which is why I’m going to end up getting two.

One thing about all of these monitors, except the ultrawide: they have a pretty small profile for what they are. They all have built-in power supplies, so you’re not getting a chunky brick on the cable. It’s one kettle cord. For driving 5K and even 6K, I was really impressed at the small footprint, though it could be that I’m coming from the LG, which is a beast: wide, but also super deep. If you’re aesthetic-conscious or don’t have a ton of space on your desk, that’s worth considering.

## Which one should you get?

The biggest takeaway from this little journey is that the perfect Mac monitor basically doesn’t exist right now. Unless you want to spend an insane amount of money, you have to figure out which trade-off you can live with. What I really want is a 32-inch 6K that’s glossy, Mac friendly, and reasonably priced, and that doesn’t exist. For me, I was willing to give up the 6K and 32-inch combo for the sake of really good colors and a really crisp display, and then doubling that up. I think it’s going to be the perfect workflow for me.

- **If you have the money,** go for the Apple monitors. You will not be disappointed. The Apple Studio Display is the industry standard for colors, brightness, consistency and longevity. It’s built really well and works perfectly with a Mac, and if you prefer glossy and you’re used to your MacBook’s built-in display, it’ll feel really good. But you spend extra for a matte finish, and extra for the stand, so it definitely adds up.
- **If you want the most productivity-focused option,** you don’t have a huge budget, and you care a little about color but not too much about text clarity, the LG, or something like it, might be your best bet. There are tons of good ultrawides now, and most are color accurate. If you’re not a gamer, I’d lean toward the ones that aren’t marketing 330 Hz. That’s where you trade off a higher refresh rate against a better quality image.
- **If you want a 32-inch 6K** and don’t want to buy from Apple or spend that much, no judgment, get the ProArt 32-inch. If you work in a really bright environment like an office, or you have a ton of studio lights, you want to avoid reflections, and you care about accurate colors, either ProArt will work, but I’d still go for the 32-inch. It looks better than the 27 in pretty much every way. Just know that if the grain is going to get to you, it will.
- **If you’re an Apple nerd** with an iPhone or an iPad and a MacBook, and you’re switching between those displays and used to how they look, the best option I can recommend is the BenQ.
- **If you’re willing to take the risk,** you could look into the Koorui 27 or 32-inch. I’ve heard great things, but I wanted to upgrade now, and the BenQ is the best solution that works for me.

I rarely do this kind of in-depth tech product review. It’s not really what this is about, but I did so much research, and I know how hard it is to find a good monitor if you’re any type of creative, whether a designer, a video editor or whatever it might be.
