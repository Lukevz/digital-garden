# How to Separate Work From Home: The 9-5 Reset Guide

[How to Separate Work From Home: The 9-5 Reset Guide](https://youtu.be/pvRL2UrLrYs)

If you feel stuck in the work from home routine, like no matter what you do you can never really mentally clock out, maybe you’re checking Slack at 8:00 PM just because your laptop’s open, there’s a good chance the boundaries between your work and personal life have gotten so blurred it starts to feel more like you’re living at work than working from home.

When you mix your personal space with your workplace, it’s tough to maintain different zones. Without a physical commute there’s no clear signal for your brain that the workday has ended, and a lot of that stress starts to pour over into the evening.

So this is my work from home reset, and some of the techniques I’ve developed over the years as a 9-to-5 employee. I generally try to stick to that schedule, but there are definitely times where a call runs late or I get pulled in early, and it can really disrupt my routine. That’s exactly when I need to lean on these even more.

One of the main things with burnout isn’t that you’re just working harder, it’s that you don’t have enough time to recover. And I’ve found that it’s not hours on the couch doomscrolling and watching TV that actually feels restful. It’s having a clear division, physically and digitally, between your work and personal life.

## 1. The five-minute brain dump

One of the biggest reasons you feel stressed after work is that you haven’t actually stopped working. There are probably a dozen conversations you had, open tasks, or ideas you forgot to write down. All of those open loops wear on you if they don’t have a place to go.

So five minutes before I clock out, I turn on a dictation app ([Wispr Flow](https://wisprflow.ai) on my Mac) and talk through everything I got done, any thoughts still going on in my head, and then I set one clear thing I want to focus on tomorrow. It’s really good at fixing mistakes and punctuation and formatting it nicely. For me it does two things: it clears out my work thoughts for the day, and it sets up the next morning, so when I sit down at my desk I know exactly what I want to work on.

This doesn’t have to be digital. You can write it out with pen and paper, and in some ways paper is more tangible. Check tomorrow’s calendar, write down the three big things for the morning, list any random clutter. You just want to get to a point where your brain feels safe letting go of those thoughts for the day.

## 2. Physical separation

As soon as I’ve wrapped up my brain dump, I make a big effort to get out of this room, and ideally out of the house. We’re trying to recreate the commute, giving our brain 20 to 30 minutes to unwind and physically separate from the environment. It’s okay if you don’t leave the house, but the number one thing is not going directly to the couch or lying down in bed. Nine times out of ten when I do that, even if I start scrolling Instagram, I end up gravitating back to my work email or opening Slack to check for extra messages. My nervous system is still in work mode because I haven’t given myself enough buffer time.

If your desk is in a guest bedroom or isn’t in a dedicated space, do something that’s a visual cue for your brain that work is over. It could literally be putting a blanket over your monitor. I did that for a couple of months a few years back, I actually put a sleeve over my whole monitor, and it was an easy way to know I shouldn’t be working or looking at that space. You could also unplug your laptop and put it on a shelf, or in a drawer, bag or closet, so it’s out of sight. Whatever the ritual is, pick something simple that’s easy to keep up every day.

## 3. Change your clothes

This might sound incredibly simple, but when you used to go to an office you were typically dressed a little differently than you would be at home. When I worked at a large corporation I definitely had dedicated work clothes, and it made things easier because as soon as I got home I wanted to change out of them. I associated those specific pants and dress shirts with the office and rarely wore them for personal occasions. The moment I got home I was throwing on shorts or sweatpants.

I don’t typically dress up for work these days, so I don’t keep assigned work clothes. But whatever brings you comfort, maybe it’s changing your socks, or throwing on a different shirt, switching it out makes it feel like you’re starting a whole new segment of your day.

## 4. Get some exercise

If I had to give you one thing to focus on from this whole list, it would be getting enough movement throughout your day. One of my favorite quotes is “emotion is created by motion.” If you want to change how you feel, you have to change how you move.

Working from home typically means sitting in the same place all day. If you can’t get around during the day, whether that’s going to a coffee shop or working at the kitchen table, you must try to make it up at the end of the day. I try to get at least 30 minutes of walking in, and a couple of days a week I’m also lifting. Being able to turn my brain off for that block, engage my body and move my muscles, almost always makes me feel better by the end of the session.

These first four are something I’ve created a habit stack out of. I finish work with my brain dump, immediately change into gym clothes, and go down to the gym for at least 30 to 45 minutes. It sounds like a lot more work when I list each one, but if you have a clear start and a clear end goal, the others fall into place because you’re building momentum. I found that if I force myself to change clothes before sitting down on the couch, I’m a lot more likely to go to the gym. And if you keep that transition as short as possible, ideally a minute or 90 seconds, your brain doesn’t have enough time to talk you out of it. To be clear, I don’t do this every single day. The point is to walk through some techniques so you can find what works for you.

## 5. Digital boundaries

If you don’t defend your attention, someone else will steal it. On my iPhone I’ve set up focus modes. One runs from about 8:30 AM to 5:30 PM, a 30-minute buffer on each end of my workday, and I only get notifications from work. All my personal stuff is muted. After 5:30 it switches to a personal mode, and I get notifications from friends and family and the other apps I want. Then at 10 PM it switches to a night mode where basically the whole screen goes black and all my notifications are off.

I’ve tied specific wallpapers to each of these modes. During the day my phone is super minimal and all the icons are grayscaled out, so I don’t feel tempted to open apps or look at notification badges. You don’t want to get distracted by personal notifications during work hours, but give yourself the same luxury on your own time of not getting pinged by work. You don’t need to see a 9 PM email instantly. Protect your peace.

## 6. The weekly review

This builds on the brain dump. You want a reflection time, ideally at least 10 or 15 minutes, to look over the week and see what loose ends you still have. Even if you’ve been doing your daily wrap-ups, when Saturday and Sunday roll around your brain is going to start thinking about Monday and that presentation you have next week. The goal is to get ahead of that by setting yourself up for the next week. It stops the Sunday scaries, and it’s how I close the week to open the weekend.

I ask myself questions like:

- What went well?
- What didn’t go well?
- What tasks am I dreading next week?
- What am I proud of?
- What ideas do I have?
- What other practical tasks haven’t I finished yet?

I take all the tasks and emails and set a reminder for Monday morning, when I sit down to plan the week. That really helps me feel like I’ve made progress and done everything I can until Monday rolls around. If a thought does pop up on Saturday, like “oh, I really need to take care of this thing,” odds are I’ve already planned time for it next week.

If you’re struggling to figure out what to focus on, think about what you feel on a Sunday evening when Monday’s about to roll around. There are parts of all of our jobs that we hate, but you want to focus on what you can control, because if you can’t control it, there’s no point stressing about it and giving up your personal time.

It’s always helped me to talk through things as a means of processing, so dictation really helps me get everything out of my head rather than just looking at what’s on screen and taking a few notes. If you need to hit record for 15 or 20 minutes and talk through every possible thought, that’s where AI can help organize things for you.

## 7. Weekend mode

It might seem counterintuitive to take action in order to relax. However you want to unwind is up to you, but I’ve noticed that avoiding certain things is what’s most helpful here.

One of the things I look forward to every weekend is not setting an alarm. I get up at the same time every day, Monday through Friday, so on Friday night, when I don’t have to wear my Apple Watch and get buzzed at 7 AM, it’s a really nice, freeing feeling to start my morning knowing I’m already on personal time. Even small things like starting the day in a different room help break the repetition.

For me this extends to apps as well. I really try to avoid apps like Notion, or even Gmail, which my company uses, so I’m not mixing personal apps with work apps. Even if you use Gmail for your personal address, download another app that lets you add just your personal account, because it’s so easy to open something for personal and then see a notification or a badge that sucks you back into thinking about work.

What’s really helped me for the weekend is always trying to do some kind of outdoor activity. It could be a long walk, going out for a meal, or a day trip somewhere. Just leaving the space is important, and if you can’t, try to stay out of the same office. It’ll break a lot of the repetition that comes with working from home. Novelty slows time down, and sameness makes years disappear.

## Start with one

You don’t need to do all seven. Work-life balance at home takes intention, so pick one, maybe changing your clothes or leaving your workspace, and start there. I’d love to hear what tips and techniques you’ve developed to help separate work from personal if you work from home.
