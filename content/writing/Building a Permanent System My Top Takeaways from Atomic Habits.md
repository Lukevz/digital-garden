# Building a Permanent System: My Top Takeaways from Atomic Habits

[Building a Permanent System: My Top Takeaways from Atomic Habits](https://youtu.be/I8q_oUN3YgU)

If you’ve ever felt stuck, like no matter what you’re doing you come out at the end of the year pretty much the same person, or you thought you’d be way further along on your goals than you currently are, there’s a good chance it comes down to the system or the habits you’ve been cultivating.

I recently finished reading *Atomic Habits*, which is all about building a sustainable system from the ground up. One of my favorite quotes is that we do not rise to the level of our goals, we fall to the level of our systems. If you’re not evaluating all the small decisions and actions you take every single day, there’s a good chance that no matter how hard you try, you’re not going to end up where you want to be.

I got a lot out of this book. I even went so far as to make a single poster that sums up all of the quotes and techniques, as a cheat sheet I can look at every day. I’ve tried to distill my thoughts into three sections: identity, environment, and systems.

## 1. Identity over outcomes

Early in the book, what really jumped out at me is the idea that you’re building the *who* and not necessarily the *what*. There are a lot of books and systems out there constantly trying to get you to make your first million, always tying you to a specific outcome. I haven’t really heard other books talk about focusing on who you want to become, and how, as a result of that, you get an internal drive to achieve those results.

So instead of saying “I want to hit 100,000 subscribers on YouTube this year,” you might say “I’m going to record one video a week.” Now you’re focusing on the inputs. What can I actually control? I could create a hundred videos this year and still not hit 100,000 subscribers, and the downside is that my happiness is now tied to that result. If I don’t hit the goal, I don’t feel like a success. But if I rewrite it as “I want 100 videos uploaded by the end of 2026” and I hit 87, that’s a pretty clear marker that I was 87 percent done. Not everything is going to be measurable like that, but the point is that you’re not tying your satisfaction and identity to something outside your control.

The system is the success. You’re successful because your system ran today, not because you reached some ten-year goal.

This leads you to look at small wins as evidence for who you’re becoming. One of my favorite quotes is that every action you take is a vote for the person you want to become. Rather than saying “I walk 7,500 steps every day,” you start to view yourself as a healthy person and say “I walk because I am a healthy person.” You aren’t just doing a photowalk, you’re becoming a photographer. I love this bird’s eye view of the big picture, because it keeps you focused on identity and not just the goal.

The other side of this is action over motion. A lot of people get caught up in the noise and feel busy, but they’re not actually making progress. It could sound like reading a ton of books on how to be a great photographer, when the action is actually going outside and using your camera. There’s always a time and a place for research and preparation, but there’s also the action, and what you actually accomplished at the end of the day. In tech and AI especially it’s easy to get lost in the noise, so focus on the high-leverage actions.

## 2. Environment design

Number two is all about designing an environment that helps support you in the moment. There’s a lot of good, practical advice here, but the thing that stood out most to me is the law of least effort. When you’re building a healthy habit, remove as much friction as you can. If you want to be at the gym at 7 AM, setting out your gym clothes the night before means that when the alarm goes off and you don’t want to get out of bed, everything is taken care of besides actually getting out of bed. The more obstacles you can remove in the moment, the less you have to rely on willpower. For me that’s also prepping my camera gear the night before.

It can work the opposite way too, with what he calls commitment devices, where you add intentional friction to a bad habit. For me that looks like removing social media from my phone, so I can really only get to it on my computer or my iPad, which I don’t always have with me. And rather than just trying to remove a bad habit, you can replace it with something good. I’m trying to get better at scrapbooking, so rather than doomscrolling, I can remove the apps from my phone *and* have a permanent space set up in our apartment where I can grab the scrapbooking materials and jump right in. You’re adding friction to the bad and removing it for the good.

He also talks about creating specific zones for your habits, loosely: one room, one use. I work from home and this is absolutely tough to do when you’re so overlapped with your work, but I generally only work at my desk in this corner of our apartment. If I’m working on something personal like scrapbooking, I’m going to do it at the kitchen table, somewhere out of the way of other use cases. It’s obviously tough in a small space, but it can also look like using different devices for different uses: an iPad for entertainment, and your phone as more of a productivity tool.

If you really have to use the same space and the same devices, another thing I try to do is reset from that environment. At the end of the workday I might change into gym clothes, and ideally I leave the apartment for at least 20 to 30 minutes, so that when I come back I feel like I’ve had enough time to decompress. I put together a whole [9-5 reset guide](/#writing/how-to-separate-work-from-home-the-9-5-reset-guide) on this.

## 3. Sustainable systems

At the end of the day, no system and no person is perfect. Life will get in the way, and you’re going to miss specific days or habits. The most important thing is probably that you’re kind to yourself, and that you’re not beating yourself up and adding artificial pressure, because then you start to associate all of those bad emotions with anything you’re trying to build for yourself.

That’s where the never skip twice rule comes in. You might miss the gym on Wednesday, but then on Thursday you’re back. If you skip twice it becomes three times, and the habit starts to lose momentum. The goal is a permanent system, not a perfect streak.

There are a lot of practical tips in the book about building momentum, like habit stacking, where you add a new habit on top of one you already have. I make coffee every morning, and it takes a couple of minutes. That’s when I journal. So making coffee becomes the cue: I pull out my phone and start dictating and brain dumping into my journal app. Pair that with a dedicated place, roughly the same time every morning, and slowly you’ve built a routine where you get up, make coffee, sit on the couch and journal, and it becomes second nature.

The important part is not doing too much all at once. There’s a concept of standardizing before you optimize, where it’s more important to just show up and check it off in the early days, because that’s when it’s hardest. You have a lot of motivation, maybe it’s the start of a new year and you’re riding that high, but sure enough, a couple of weeks in you start to find reasons not to show up. This year I really wanted to get back into journaling. But rather than saying I’d write for 30 minutes every day, I said I’d journal on the morning of every weekday. I didn’t worry about weekends, and I didn’t put any limits or goals on how long or wordy the entries needed to be. It was more important that I woke up and journaled before I started work. Down the road I might add specific questions, or aim for five or ten minutes, but I’m moving slowly because I don’t want to do too much at once.

Systems can absolutely be changed, but if you’re spending more time editing the system than operating inside of it, it can end up being glorified procrastination. I used to struggle with this a lot with digital note-taking. In the world of Notion, where you can build whatever you want, a second brain, the perfect note-taking system, I got so sucked into building templates and automations and making my dashboard pretty that I wasn’t actually taking notes or getting anything done.

That said, there’s absolutely a time to review a system that isn’t working. You might be trying to build too many habits at once. Maybe you just need to pick your top three focuses for the next 90 days. No system will be perfect because our goals are constantly evolving, which is why it’s great to track your inputs. You might say you want to read 50 books in a year, get halfway through, and realize you’ve read 20 or 30 books and don’t remember anything. That’s a point to say, I’d rather read one book a month and actually retain it. Because you tracked the inputs, you have the freedom to change them without feeling like you’re falling short of 50 books.

The book calls blind repetition a trap. If all you’re ever doing is showing up and it’s no longer providing value, it might just not be working anymore, and you can decide to change it. The key is that you’ve already had a consistent run. You didn’t try it a couple of times and give up. Use your best judgment: I’ve done this for 90 days, I don’t think it works for me anymore, and I’m going to try something different. Build that grace for yourself. Use reviews and habit trackers as data to refine your habits and align them with your current values, not as a judgment scorecard.

## Try it

Like I said, there’s a lot of practical advice, and it’s a really easy read in my opinion. I got the audiobook and binged it in about two weeks, which is pretty fast for me. I listened to a chapter every time I was at the gym. He also makes it easy to follow along with chapter summaries and even section summaries. I’d highly recommend it if you struggle to build habits, especially if you’ve had a bit of a negative relationship with yourself around discipline and how you go about improving your lifestyle.

If you want a simple way to try this out, look at your habits and ask which one is no longer providing value for you. And if you’re working on a new habit, try to identify the gateway habit. If your end goal is to document your day, open up the notes app and write one sentence. Find the entry point that takes less than two minutes and can help you start building momentum.
