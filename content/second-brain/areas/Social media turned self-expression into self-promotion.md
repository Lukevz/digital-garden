Social media used to be self-expression and somewhere along the way it became self-promotion, and that's what pushed me into digital gardening. I've been making videos for over 14 years. My early stuff was cringe, but it was free: no SEO, no content strategy, just making things because I wanted to, see [[My career runs from YouTube tutorials to Instinct]]. Then everything needed a hook, a niche, and a growth plan.

As a multi-interest creative that shift made the internet exhausting instead of energizing. I was spending more time performing than exploring. What I wanted back was a place online that felt like my old creative self: messy, playful, low stakes.

That's the gap a garden fills. A blog is polished and finished, you publish and it's done. A garden is drafts, notes, and half-formed thoughts that stay editable forever, so it's less about lowering quality and more about lowering the pressure, which is the argument in [[A digital garden is a philosophy not a website]]. Over time it becomes a map of your thinking instead of a feed. I wrote the full case at /#writing/5-reasons-you-need-a-digital-garden-with-examples--tools-to-help.

It also changed how I treat the platforms themselves. Getting social media off my phone is one of the habits in [[In 2026 I am building habits that regulate instead of optimize]], and YouTube is now the one place I still publish deliberately, see [[I run two YouTube channels]].
