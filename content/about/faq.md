## Q — Who are you?

I'm Luke. Designer in Atlanta. I design software for veterinarians at Instinct, and I keep a digital garden at lukevz.com where I think out loud.

## Q — What do you do for work?

I'm a senior product designer at Instinct — we build software for veterinarians. My specialty is design systems, which is where most of my career has been focused.

## Q — How long have you been designing?

About 12–13 years, depending on how you count. I started making YouTube tutorials and doing freelance web and graphic design work around 2012–2013. PwC was where I went deep on UX and design systems professionally.

## Q — What tools do you use?

Figma is my main design tool — I ran a full department migration to it at PwC. Bear for notes. I build side projects in vanilla HTML/CSS/JS.

## Q — How do you take notes?

I use Bear, and I keep a digital garden as the public layer. The site you're on is the garden — it's a Bear-style note browser with public-facing notes.

## Q — What books should I read?

I keep a bookshelf at /bookshelf. The two that genuinely shaped how I think: *Creativity, Inc.* — it's about Pixar but it's really about how to build a culture where good work can happen. And *The Speed of Trust* — changed how I think about relationships, teams, and decisions. If I could only hand someone two books, those are it.

## Q — What's your design process?

I always start by defining the baseline — what are people doing today to solve this problem? If they're in a spreadsheet, I know what that feels like, and I know I can't deliver something worse than that. From there it's about asking the right questions before touching any design tools: What's the problem? Who's it for? Why? Does it even need to be solved, and does it need to be solved by us right now? Most projects skip that and pay for it later.

## Q — What's your take on design systems?

That's my specialty — I've spent most of my career building and managing them. The thing people get wrong is thinking a design system is a component library. It's not. It's a shared language. The components are just the output. The real work is the decisions behind them, the documentation, and getting people to actually adopt it.

## Q — What do you think about AI in design?

AI is an accelerator. It amplifies your existing process — it doesn't fix a bad one. If you already think well and work well, you should see exponential return. The thing that's underappreciated is what it does to the value of taste. When tools can execute for almost anyone, the differentiator becomes judgment. You have to keep training your eye and honing your craft. Never outsource your thinking.

## Q — What's your aesthetic / visual taste?

Minimal and clean, generally. I have a soft spot for skeuomorphism — I think the iOS 7 pivot was too sharp and designers should have pushed back more. I genuinely love liquid glass right now. The rollout had problems but the boldness of trying something completely new deserves credit. Apple always gets that from me.

## Q — What do you wish more designers understood?

Two things: Think like a business owner, not an order-taker. Ask how someone got to the problem, not just solve the one they handed you. And don't get attached to your work — we're delivering something for someone else, and it's totally fine to throw it all away and start over, or to get critical feedback on something you're proud of. Both are just part of the job.

## Q — Why did you leave PwC / why Instinct?

PwC was an incredible academy — fast-paced, huge variety, and I'm grateful for the network and experience. But at that scale you can start to feel like a cog, and I'd rather be close to the published version of my work and actually see the impact. Instinct is smaller, freer, and the work directly helps veterinarians, who are overworked and stressed. Reducing that stress is what drives my design decisions now.

## Q — Why do you keep a digital garden?

It's more of a philosophy than a website to me. A digital garden lowers the barrier to sharing something publicly, but it also holds you accountable to closing threads. As a neurodivergent, it's helpful to let your brain go wild and follow what catches your eye — but you can also trap yourself bookmarking, reading, consuming, and never creating. The garden is the antidote to that.

The key thing that makes it different from a blog or YouTube or Instagram is that it's not project-based. It's thought-based. Thoughts are ephemeral, they bleed into each other, and you can make connections all over the place. There's no well-crafted package to ship. You just put it out there.

## Q — What's the 52 project?

52 is a local markdown-based note-taking app I'm building. The idea is to stop outsourcing your thinking to AI and get back to that feeling of being in control you get with pen and paper — just focused on taking good notes again, one work week at a time.

The core is a notes surface where you capture meetings and ideas. Those can be turned into action items, which automatically get tracked on a tasks page. You can tag collaborators and stakeholders, then filter by person to pull up everything about them quickly. Achievements and milestones can be turned into "epochs" so you can zoom out from the week-to-week and see your actual impact over time. It's also essentially a live CV — you're building the record as you go instead of scrambling to reconstruct it when you need to make a career move.
