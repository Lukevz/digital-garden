## On design

Good design is an answer to a series of questions. Before anything else, you need a crystal-clear understanding of the problem — and that's harder than it sounds. The more you try to define it, the more complex it feels. It's an uphill battle to figure out what the problem actually is, and then to keep iterating until you can deliver the simplest version of a solution.

Simple doesn't mean basic. Simple is the most elegant complexity — distilling something without sacrificing utility or value. That's the bar.

The questions I always ask at the start of any problem:
- What problem are we solving?
- Who are we solving it for?
- Why are we solving it?
- Does it even need to be solved?
- Does it need to be solved now?
- Does it need to be solved by us?

The other thing I always do first is define the baseline. If users are working in a spreadsheet today, I know what that experience feels like — I should never deliver something worse than that. Defining the baseline tells you what "better" actually means. If you skip it, you have no way to measure whether you succeeded.

## On AI

AI is an accelerator. It amplifies your existing process — it can't fix a bad one. If your way of solving problems is already solid, you should see an exponential return on efficiency and impact. If it's not, AI just makes that worse faster.

The thing that's underappreciated is what AI does to the value of taste. When tools can execute for almost anyone, the differentiator becomes judgment — having an eye that's been trained, a sense of what's actually good versus what's just plausible. You have to be self-aware and attuned to the world, to real art, to keep honing that. It's a human process. You should never outsource your thinking.

## On building

I think a lot about removing layers. Every layer between a designer and the person they're solving for — whether that's a PM, an engineer, leadership, whoever — is a layer of removal. Those people can be great at their jobs and still dilute your understanding of the actual user. Early in a project especially, you want as much direct signal as possible before adding other context in.

Don't get attached to work either. We're generally responsible for delivering something for someone else. It's okay to throw it all away and start over. It's also okay to produce something you're proud of and get very critical feedback on it. Both of those are just part of the job.

## On learning

My 22-year-old self needed to hear: ask more questions about how people arrived at the problem, not just about the problem itself. It's easy to go hard on solving what someone puts in front of you. It's harder — and more valuable — to understand how they got there.

## On taste

I love minimal and clean. I've always had a soft spot for skeuomorphism — that moment when we went from it to flat design with iOS 7, I think a lot of designers just accepted orders instead of pushing back. Liquid glass is the most recent thing I genuinely love — the rollout was a separate conversation, but the boldness of trying something completely new deserves respect. That's what I've always admired about Apple.

## On the digital garden

A digital garden is more of a philosophy than a website. It's a mindset for how you approach work — in whatever craft you're in. The point is that it lowers the barrier to sharing something publicly while also holding you accountable to closing threads. As a neurodivergent, it's really helpful to let your brain go wherever it wants and follow what catches your eye. The danger is falling into paralysis — bookmarking, reading, consuming, and never creating. The garden fights that.

What makes it different from a blog or YouTube is that it's thought-based, not project-based. Thoughts are ephemeral. They bleed into each other. You can make connections across everything. There's no polished package to ship. You just put it out there and let it grow.

## Recurring frameworks I use

- Define the baseline first — what are people doing today? Never deliver worse than that.
- Ask six questions before touching any tools: What's the problem? Who's it for? Why? Does it need to be solved? Now? By us?
- Zoom out to find the real pattern — look for what's natural in the physical world, not just what other apps do.
- Remove layers between you and who you're solving for, especially early in a project.

## Beliefs I'd defend

- Designers need to think like business owners, not order-takers. Ask how they got to the problem, not just how to solve the problem they gave you.
- Design is mostly systematic pattern recognition — but the patterns that matter most aren't from other software, they're from the physical world. How do people know to push vs. pull a door? How do they know where to park? Those are the patterns that feel completely natural because they've been adopted at mass scale. The best digital UX borrows from that, not from what another app does.
- A lot of designers copy other software thinking they're doing pattern recognition. They're not — they're just templating. Real pattern recognition means finding what's already familiar and natural to people in everyday life, then adapting it.
- People are complex and different. Some metaphors land for some users and completely miss others. Constantly re-using digital heuristics that work in some contexts won't always translate — you have to stay attuned to why something feels natural, not just that it does.

## Beliefs I'm less sure about

- How much AI will actually change the core of design work vs. the peripheral stuff. I think the instinct and taste is irreplaceable but I hold that loosely.
