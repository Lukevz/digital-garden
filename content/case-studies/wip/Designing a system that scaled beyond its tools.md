# Designing a system that scaled beyond its tools
Over four years at PwC, I helped turn the global design system from a Sketch library into infrastructure that 3,000+ products and 120 designers depended on. The work spanned a full toolchain migration, a patterns initiative, a community marketplace, a brand-led visual refresh, and the documentation and team practices that held it together. The through-line: a small team can't govern everything, so the system itself has to do the scaling.

## The problem
When I started leading work on the design system, the team supported a sprawling product portfolio with a tool stack that was actively in the way — Sketch for design, Abstract for versioning, InVision for prototyping, Zeplin for handoff. Designers spent meaningful time stitching files between four tools. Engineers got specs through a fifth surface. And the design system itself lived in Sketch libraries that were increasingly hard to keep in sync across hundreds of product teams.
Behind the tooling problem was a deeper one: a 30-person design system org cannot meaningfully govern 3,000+ products. Anything we shipped had to either scale on its own or hand control to the people closer to the work. Most "design system at scale" failure modes I'd seen came from teams trying to centralize too much and bottlenecking on themselves. I wanted to avoid that.

## The work
**Moving the org to Figma.** Before there was any departmental commitment to switching tools, I built the business case and pitched it to partners and leadership — framing it around collaboration, cost (consolidating four tools into one), and where the industry was heading. The pitch landed, and over the next ~18 months we phased Sketch out entirely.
I rebuilt the majority of the design system in Figma myself during the v4 beta and was the design lead on one of the first two products to ship fully in the new system. From there I led the migration for the rest of the org — 120 designers — which meant writing 30–40 pages of documentation and running weekly Figma office hours for six months. The office hours started with 30–50 people and a curriculum I'd developed; by month five, attendance had dropped to a handful and Figma-related questions in the broader team channels were going quiet. That was the signal to wind them down and fold the residual conversation into our existing biweekly UX knowledge share. The drop wasn't disengagement — it was that people no longer needed the help.

**The patterns initiative.** With the tooling unblocked, I led a parallel effort to build out shared patterns for the things every product was reinventing — login screens, table layouts, data filtering sidebars and headers, and roughly 40 others. The framing mattered: patterns were never meant to be drop-in solutions. They were starting points with documented accessibility guidance, configurable variants, and clear handoff to React components owned by engineering.
The research was substantial. For each pattern I audited where it appeared across the portfolio, talked to designers and engineers who'd built variations, and pulled help center tickets to find friction points. Then I pitched in weekly design reviews to PMs, the managing director, and other design directors before anything got built. Engineers pushed back on implementation details more than once, and the patterns were better for it. The point wasn't to eliminate tickets — it was to give 3,000 products a shared starting point so consistency didn't depend on the system team policing every screen.

**The Marketplace.** Patterns we shipped officially had to work across the whole portfolio. But a lot of useful work was more specific — a clever component one team built, a layout that solved a problem in one domain. Rather than force everything through central governance, we built a community Marketplace where teams could publish their own components and templates. If something got real traction there, we'd consider promoting it into the official system.
This was a deliberate split-governance call: official system for things that needed to scale to 3,000 products, Marketplace for the long tail. The governance model worked. The submission model didn't — more on that below.

**The "new era" brand refresh.** Toward the end of my time, PwC committed to a brand refresh that introduced glass morphism across the system. I partnered with the design lead on the system rebuild and worked directly with Global Brand, who owned the new color palette and logo but weren't product or UI designers. Glass meant layering, which meant readability and contrast risk. Rather than handle that case-by-case, I pushed to solve it at the system level — building a variable architecture that gave designers control over gradient opacity and transparency, and a dedicated high-contrast mode for both light and dark themes. That meant any team using the system could ship the brand refresh without re-litigating accessibility. Six or seven products picked it up as POCs before I left; the rollout continued after.

**The team and the playbooks.** I was leading the patterns work, the Figma transition, the office hours, and design on other projects before I was formally promoted to manager. Once the title caught up, I started directly mentoring the three designers on the team — weekly design workshops where we'd review work, give feedback, and practice pitching projects before the biweekly design review with leadership.
The piece I'm proudest of from the management work is the playbook hub. Thirty to forty single-page SOPs covering the actual lifecycle of design system work — exploration, presenting designs, writing tickets, incorporating accessibility feedback, prepping for handoff, auditing developed implementations, giving feedback to engineers. One-pagers were the constraint; they had to be easy to maintain or they wouldn't survive. Alongside that I built out a Design System Champions list — PMs, designers, and stakeholders across the org who wanted to give feedback but weren't on the team, giving us a standing test audience without expanding headcount.

## What changed
The Figma migration finished. All 120 designers moved, Sketch was retired, and the four-tool stack collapsed to one. The drop-off in office hours attendance and Figma questions across the org was the cleanest signal that the upskilling had taken — people stopped needing help because they were past needing it.
The patterns work shifted how new products got built. Login screens, filtering, and data tables stopped being one-off design exercises. The Marketplace gave teams a place to share work that didn't need to be globally governed, and a small number of those submissions did get traction and get pulled into the official system.
The brand refresh shipped on a system architecture designed for it, not retrofitted to it. The high-contrast mode was built in from the start rather than added under pressure later, which mattered because Global Brand's approval cycles were slow — going back for a second pass would have stalled everyone.
The playbooks and Champions program kept a 4-person team from being the bottleneck on a system serving thousands of products. That was the real outcome: not any single artifact, but a working model for governing scale with a small team.

## In hindsight
The Marketplace is the thing I'd do differently. We launched it with the right intent — let teams contribute outside central governance — but we wrapped it in too much review process. The team committed to personally verifying every submission, which felt responsible but became the bottleneck the Marketplace was supposed to bypass. The barrier to entry, especially for code submissions, was high enough that only a handful of people actually contributed. Some of that was Big Four red tape we couldn't move. A lot of it was a decision we made.
If I were doing it again, I'd launch with the inverse default: anyone can contribute, nothing is verified by us, and verification is a signal teams can earn rather than a gate they have to pass. It took us months to work that out, and the Marketplace never fully recovered the momentum we lost in those months.
